Held by the Bamboo Bow

In almost every other way of making a picture, you begin by putting down what you want to be seen. In katazome (型染) and Okinawan bingata (紅型), you begin by painting what you want to keep blank.

Before a single drop of indigo, gamboge, or cinnabar touches the cloth, you lay a hand-carved mulberry-paper stencil—darkened and toughened with aged persimmon tannin—over the raw, unbleached hemp linen. With a wooden spatula, you press sticky glutinous rice paste through the open cutouts of the stencil. Wherever the paste settles into the weave, the linen is sealed off from everything that comes next. The wing feathers of the crane, the veins of the seven-pointed maple leaves, the concentric foam arches of the waves, and the constellations of tiny arare hailstone dots are all drawn first in invisible mud.

Only after the paste has dried into a firm crust do you stretch the bolt out in the sun on shinshi (伸子)—slender, springy splints of split bamboo fitted with three-pronged brass needles at either end. You hook the needles into the left and right selvedges and bow each bamboo splint until it snaps flat across the width of the fabric, pulling the linen drum-tight in the open air so the brush can work without bleeding across the underside.

Then comes surikomi bokashi (摺込ぼかし): rubbing mineral pigments ground in soybean milk into the open cells between the walls of dried paste with stubby deer-hair brushes, shading each chrysanthemum petal and maple lobe from bare cream at the base to vermilion, plum, or malachite at the tip. While the cloth is still pinned to the bamboo bows—like the lower-right corner here, where the raised gray-ochre crust of dried rice paste is still clinging to the wave arcs—the textile looks muted and earthy, half-buried under its own scaffolding. It is only when the bamboo bows are unhooked and the bolt is carried down to the cold river that the rice paste softens, lifts away in the current, and every hidden cream-white line wakes up at once to hold the colors apart.
