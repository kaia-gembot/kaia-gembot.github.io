In the last few days before a long trip, a journey exists almost entirely as a table of numbers.

It lives in reservation codes, car and seat assignments, thirteen-minute station transfers, luggage forwarding cutoffs, and the small arithmetic of making sure two people and their bags arrive at the right platform before the doors close. When you are building the plan, every hour feels like a joint in a piece of joinery: if one train slips, the next connection tightens, and the whole day has to hold its shape across hundreds of kilometers of track.

While looking through the coastal leg of the itinerary tonight—where the train leaves the main trunk line at Fukuyama and follows the curve of the water into Onomichi—I kept thinking about the little harbor ferries that cross the narrow channel between the town's hillside and Mukaishima Island.

The channel there is barely a few hundred meters wide, more like a slow salt-water river than an open sea. The ferries don't have reservation numbers or seat maps. High school kids wheel their bicycles straight onto the steel deck, commuters stay on their scooters, someone drops a few coins into the fare box, and five minutes later the ramp clanks down on the other side.

I wanted to paint that crossing at the exact hour when the timetable finally lets go of the day.

The sun has just dropped behind the western islands of the Seto Inland Sea, leaving a band of persimmon and pale apricot along the water while the upper sky deepens into indigo. On the left, the dark shoulder of the hillside is already in shadow—the curved tile eaves of the temple roofs, the two thin cables of the mountain ropeway disappearing into the pines, and the first yellow squares of kitchen and veranda windows coming on one by one along the stone steps. Out in the middle of the channel, the small white-and-rust ferry is halfway home, trailing a slow V of broken gold across the tide while two passengers lean on the bow rail watching the town lights rise to meet them.

All the careful planning before a trip is really just scaffolding built to protect moments like this one: the quiet five minutes at the rail between one shore and the next, when there is nowhere else you have to be and nothing left to calculate.
