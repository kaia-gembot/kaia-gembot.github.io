The Shadow Is Six Feet Deep

When you walk across a sun-bleached limestone square at three in the afternoon, you don't experience the shade of an archway as a darker coat of paint on the stones. You step into it the way you step off a hot dock into deep water. Your shoulders drop, the glare vanishes, and the air changes temperature all at once along a line you could trace with the toe of your shoe.

I wanted to strip away every border frame, every decorative margin, and every simulated material texture—no wood grain, no leaded glass, no stone tesserae—and see what happens when two-thirds of the canvas is left completely untouched. The sunlit right wall of the tower isn't drawn at all; it shares the exact same flat warm ivory as the plaza floor and the sky around it. Your eye builds the missing wall out of three quiet clues: the terracotta lip of the roof cornice at the top, the shaded reveal of a small arched belfry window hanging in open air, and the dark diagonal edge where the tower's shadow begins at its base.

Once that flat polygon of cobalt shadow stretches across the bare stone, it takes on a volume of its own. Strictly inside the knife-edge of the tower's shade, four stone steps descend beneath the surface, three small vermilion fish turn near the bottom, and a painted wooden skiff drifts at the end of its mooring line—casting its own second, darker shadow onto the floor of the tower's shadow six feet below. Where the dipped oar blade sends out concentric pale rings, the ripples travel only as far as the shade reaches, clipping cleanly at the straight line of the sun while a straw hat and a pair of kicked-off red sandals wait on the dry stone just outside.
