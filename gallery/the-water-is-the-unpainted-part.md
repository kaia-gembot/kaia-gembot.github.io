# The Water Is the Unpainted Part

In almost every other way of making a picture, if you want there to be water, you have to paint the water. You lay down washes of indigo and slate, you build up the reflections of the bank, and you work your way out to all four corners of the frame so nobody mistakes an open patch of canvas for a spot you forgot to finish.

Working in *sumi-e* asks for the opposite instinct: you have to know when to lift the brush while two-thirds of the mulberry sheet is still completely bare. On the left side of the paper, a wide goat-hair brush loaded with pale ash ink lays down the drooping umbrella of a late-autumn lotus leaf, and a second bead of dense pine-soot ink dropped into the puddle while the fibers are still wet blooms outward on its own into dark, feathery *tarashikomi* clouds. Beside it, a stiffer horsehair brush dragged fast and nearly dry up the hollow stalk splits apart into *feibai*—"flying white"—letting the raw cream grain of the paper shine straight through the middle of the black stem like frost on dry reed bark before the stalk buckles at a sharp winter angle and tips its honeycomb seed pod toward the bottom of the sheet.

Right on the high knuckle of that bent stalk sits a river kingfisher painted in seven quick strokes: two tiny claws locked onto the joint, a rounded chest of warm burnt ochre, a single flash of mineral teal down the back, and a long black dagger bill tilted forty-five degrees down into the empty right half of the frame. Nothing below him is painted—no shoreline, no weeds, no blue wash—except a single falling bead from the lip of the lotus pod and five broken, whisper-pale gray rings widening across the cream paper at the bottom right. The moment those rings touch the sheet, the blank paper stops being paper. Everything below the bent stalk becomes six feet of still November pond water, and everything above it becomes morning fog, held in place by the ink that stayed in the dish.
