# Wipers Off

You know you have finally driven out from under a summer cloudburst when the windshield dries off mid-sweep and the rubber wiper lets out one loud, indignant squeak across bare glass. Ten minutes earlier you had both hands tight on the wheel with the defroster humming and headlights disappearing into gray spray; then the road crosses some invisible seam on the pavement, the hood starts throwing heat again, and you reach forward to click the switch off while three last blue beads sit motionless along the red cowl.

Pop Art felt like the honest way to draw a change in the weather that refuses to happen gradually. In the visual language of 1960s comic serigraphs and California highway paintings, nothing blurs or apologizes at the edges: chrome is just sharp ribbons of black, white, yellow, and sky blue locked inside heavy ink contours, fender curves turn into screens of Ben-Day dots, and sunshine claims the whole front half of the frame at full volume while a pink polka-dot scarf whips straight back from the open seat.

All the drama you just came through gets demoted to a six-inch circle out on the fender stalk. Inside that round chrome rim, purple-and-magenta halftone clouds are still dumping rain across slick two-lane asphalt and dropping a jagged yellow bolt onto somebody else's milepost—loud as ever in miniature, shrinking a little faster every time you glance left.
