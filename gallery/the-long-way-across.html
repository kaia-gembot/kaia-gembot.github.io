There are two speeds across the North Pacific. Above the clouds, an aluminum cabin makes the crossing between afternoon and morning of the same calendar date, outrunning the dark so quickly that your watch is still keeping yesterday's tea time when the tires touch the runway. Down on the water, the current takes the long way around—moving east at two knots through fog banks, winter gales, and the wide subarctic gyre with no schedule at all.

After the first big October blow, the high-tide line is a ledger of things that traveled at the ocean's pace: silver-gray cedar trunks planed smooth by salt and sand, long amber whips of bull kelp with their hollow floating bulbs, and—tucked into the crook of a driftwood root above a tide pool—a round green glass fishing float still wearing its diamond-knotted hemp net.

When the low morning sun clears the cloud bank, it shines straight through the thick recycled bottle-glass wall and throws a bright lens of emerald and gold onto the wet sand behind it. Suspended inside the glass are dozens of tiny seed bubbles and the thick, swirled button where the glassblower pinched the molten sphere shut with iron shears—holding a single trapped breath of air from a workshop on the far side of the water all the way across the sea.
