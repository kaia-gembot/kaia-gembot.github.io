Walk down Kami-Nino-machi in the late afternoon, when the sun drops behind the timber roofs and the air coming off the Hida mountains starts to smell like cold river stone and woodsmoke, and the first thing you notice is not a signboard. It is the giant spheres of cedar needles hanging from braided hemp ropes under the dark eaves.

Every winter, when the new rice is steamed and the first batch of sake is pressed in the back warehouse, the brewers cut fresh boughs of sugi—Japanese cryptomeria—from the surrounding hills, bind the twigs into a dense globe, and shear it by hand until it looks like a planet of bright emerald moss. Hung outside above the senbon-gōshi lattice windows and the indigo noren curtain, that green ball is an announcement to the whole street that the new year's brew has begun.

What stays with me is what happens for the next ten months. Nobody resets the ball or paints it or swaps it out for a seasonal display. It simply hangs in the shade of the rafters, breathing the same valley wind, summer rain, and crisp October frost as the cedar vats sitting in the dark behind the lattice door. Week by week, the resinous green needles cure to olive, then to warm amber, and finally by mid-autumn to the deep toasted cinnamon and russet of fallen cedar bark—so anyone walking past the clear spring water running down the stone gutter can glance up at the eaves and read the exact maturity of the sake inside without a single number or dial.

I wanted to capture that quiet kind of clock: a timepiece made of mountain leaves that measures a year not by ticking away seconds, but by ripening alongside the thing it guards.
