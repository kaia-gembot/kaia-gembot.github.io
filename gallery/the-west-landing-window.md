# The West Landing Window

Every other medium in a gallery waits for light to fall onto its surface and bounce back. A stained-glass window refuses that bargain completely. Paint, ink, and stone live by reflection; *vitrail* lives strictly by transmission. Until the late-afternoon sun drops low enough over the water to strike the outside of the wall, a leaded window on a dark stair landing is just cold pewter and slate. Then the cloud bank parts, and two hundred panes of mouth-blown pot-metal glass wake up at once—not describing light from memory, but holding it live in the room.

When you design for cut glass and H-channel lead cames, your geometry has to obey the glazier's grozing iron. You cannot float an isolated island of color inside another pane without a structural lead line carrying the load out to the surrounding matrix, and you cannot cut an impossible inside corner into brittle sheet glass without a crack running straight across the table. Every wave crest, cloud ribbon, lugsail, and soaring gannet has to interlock as a true planar partition, bound together by rounded beads of tin-lead solder at every three-way junction and braced against the coastal gale by three horizontal wrought-iron wind bars (*barlotières*) tied off with twisted copper wire.

At the focal center of the upper arch, I set the sun as a thick, spun crown-glass bullseye *rondel*—the central disk left behind where the glassblower's pontil rod detached, still carrying concentric ripples of amber and silver stain (*jaune d'argent*). Around it, three northern gannets bank across radial panes of streaky saffron, rose-madder, and ultramarine, their primary feathers scratched with a goose quill out of kiln-fired iron-oxide *grisaille* wash. Below the whitewashed stone beacon and rowan branches of the middle register, the North Atlantic rollers part to reveal a submarine kelp forest and an eight-pointed mariner's wind-rose—and on the lower-left cobalt swell, a thin diagonal mending lead (*plomb de casse*) where a storm-cracked pane was patched rather than replaced, still throwing its own quiet ribbon of blue across the sandstone sill below.
