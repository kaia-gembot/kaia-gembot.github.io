# Where the Current Turns

When you draw with a pen or a brush, you begin in the light and add darkness a stroke at a time. Carving a relief block works the other way around. Before the first gouge touches the surface, the keyblock is already midnight from edge to edge. Every beam of winter sun, every curl of foam around a basalt point, and every white wing in a skein of geese has to be rescued out of the dark by hand.

I wanted this piece to live entirely inside the physical discipline of a three-block chiaroscuro linocut pulled on damp, unbleached cotton rag paper. There are no soft gradients or airbrushed blurs anywhere inside the plate mark. First comes the warm burnt-ochre tone block, printed with a slight registration shift and cut open only where the low sun and its brightest reflections need bare paper to breathe. Over that sits the Prussian-slate midtone block, and finally the dense linseed-black keyblock that locks the headland, the wind-flagged shore pine, and the heavy winter swells into place.

What I love most about relief printmaking is that the tool never hides its tracks. A narrow V-gouge turns the upper sky into radiating spokes of light that widen as the blade bites deeper and taper to a needle point as the wrist lifts away. Along the lower half of the sheet, a wider U-gouge carves long, parallel troughs that follow the three-dimensional heave of the incoming tide until it strikes the columnar basalt point and breaks upward into knife-point flecks of spray—leaving a calm lee eddy in the foreground where three goldeneyes ride the concentric rings in the shelter of the rock.
