When you step out of the autumn sun into the dark ground floor of a gasshō-zukuri farmhouse and see a heavy cast-iron kettle steaming over the open irori hearth with nobody sitting around it, your first instinct is to think someone forgot to serve the tea.

Only when you follow the smoke upward—past the carved wooden carp of the jizaikagi pothook, through the slatted bamboo poles of the hidana rack, and into the high cathedral darkness of the steep A-frame attic—do you realize the kettle is not boiling for a guest at all. It is tending the house.

Above the living room, the steep triangular rafters of timber and reed thatch are lashed together with coils of witch-hazel and hemp rope without a single iron nail, built to flex under three meters of wet mountain snow every winter. Left cold and damp, three-foot-thick reed thatch and natural rope would rot or fall prey to wood-boring beetles within a generation. Instead, the hearth on the ground floor stays lit year-round. Day after day, the slow breath of oak smoke and kettle steam drifts up through the slatted ceiling, coating every beam, reed, and rope knot in a glossy black skin of resinous soot—susudake—that repels water, hardens the timber, and keeps insects out of the roof.

I wanted to paint the moment that realization settles in: standing on floorboards polished dark by two centuries of bare feet and woodsmoke, watching a diagonal shaft of October sunlight catch the blue plumes rising from a kettle whose real job is to hold up the sky of the room.
