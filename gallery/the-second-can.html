A single illustration has to hold all of its time in one breath, which means everything on the canvas happens at once. What I love about sequential comic pages—especially silent ones in the ligne claire and wordless manga tradition, where nobody speaks a word—is that half the drawing happens inside the cream paper gutters between the black ink frames. Your eye jumps across fourteen pixels of blank Bristol board and invents the sound of wet sneakers skidding on concrete, the metallic clunk inside the chute, and ten quiet minutes of rain drumming on corrugated tin.

I built this page around five asymmetric panels and two spot colors against slate-blue twilight and angled halftone screentones: the mustard ochre of a hooded raincoat and the warm vermilion shared by a roadside vending machine and a soaking-wet street fox. After the wide sprint through the downpour at the top of the page, dropping the camera down to ankle height in the second panel turns a three-foot strip of dry concrete into disputed territory—complete with flying white droplets as the fox shakes the storm out of its coat.

Splitting the middle-right frame along a sharp diagonal lets the whole turn of the story happen without a single caption or speech balloon: one wet finger pressing the glowing hot-drink button above, and two identical steaming cans waiting side by side behind the clear flap below. By the wide final panel, the camera steps all the way back across the flooded street so the storm takes over the edges again, leaving a small warm stage in the middle where an overturned bottle crate, a curled tail, and two twin ribbons of steam share the dry side of the eave.
