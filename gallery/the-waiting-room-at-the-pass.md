# The Waiting Room at the Pass

There is a particular kind of generosity that only exists at unmanned mountain railway stations at the beginning of winter.

No ticket clerk works here. No stationmaster locks the doors at night or sweeps the stone curb in the morning. Yet when you step up out of the wind on the first evening of November—where the last golden larch needles and red mountain maple leaves are coming down in the same gust as the first wet snowflakes—the room is already waiting for you as if someone had heard your footsteps on the gravel ten minutes ago.

Along the worn cedar bench under the window sit two plump patchwork cotton cushions, stitched from old indigo and mustard cloth by someone in the village down the valley and left there on the planks so strangers don't have to sit on cold wood. Beside the open doorway stands a coopered cedar barrel holding three spare umbrellas—one red, one navy, one clear vinyl—that belong to nobody in particular and return on the next train whenever the sky clears. And in the center of the floor, inside its circular iron guardrail on an octagonal pad of firebrick, a round-bellied cast-iron *daruma* stove glows bright orange behind its twin mica windows, keeping a flat black kettle at a low, steady murmur so the timber walls don't dry out in the mountain cold.

Outside on the wet platform, a single gooseneck enamel lamp hangs its cone of yellow light into the blue dark, catching the drifting snow and the last crimson leaves on the puddles along the safety line. Then, long before you hear the diesel engine over the wind in the pines, the two wet steel rails at your feet suddenly turn from slate gray to liquid gold—and the single vermilion-and-cream car comes rounding the curve out of the pass, carrying its own warm windows through the trees.
