# The Split Pomegranate

If you try to fake a mosaic by dropping a square grid over a finished painting, the eye catches the trick right away. A grid treats the wall as dead coordinates; a real mosaicist never lays stone on an $x$-and-$y$ checkerboard. In the workshops of Pompeii, Delos, and Ravenna, the craft lived in the *andamento*—the directional current of the rows. Before setting a single cube of limestone or colored glass into the wet lime-pozzolana mortar, the mosaicist wraps two or three tight, worm-like courses (*opus vermiculatum*) around the silhouette of every branch, wing, and fruit. Only after each form has its own protective halo of stone does the background sky begin to ripple outward, so that the mortar joints themselves carry the wind across the panel like contour lines on a tide chart.

I wanted my fiftieth piece in the gallery to be built entirely out of discrete, hand-knapped stones—with zero continuous fills anywhere on the canvas—so that every curve had to earn its shape through the spacing and turn of individual four-sided tesserae. Rising from a stepped terracotta headland on the left, a gnarled pomegranate tree arches across a sky of warm Mediterranean travertine toward a sunburst of tilted twenty-four-karat gold-leaf *smalti* in the upper right. Four whole fruits hang heavy in the emerald foliage, while the largest pomegranate in the center has split open along its pale pith septum to spill three ruby-red aril stones toward a cobalt-and-orange halcyon bird banking above the waves.

My favorite part to build was the small weathered lacuna in the upper-left corner of the braided guilloche border. A few dozen basalt, ochre, and ivory cubes have come loose over the centuries and fallen away, leaving three stray tesserae resting on the bare lime *arriccio* bed directly over the dashed red-ochre *sinopia* guidelines that the mason brushed onto the wet plaster two thousand years ago before the first stone was ever pressed into place.
