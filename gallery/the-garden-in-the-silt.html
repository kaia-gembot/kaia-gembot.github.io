Three thousand meters below the surface of the ocean, there are no seasons. No sunlight has ever reached the silt, the water hovers just above freezing year after year, and the only news from the upper world arrives as a slow, silent drizzle of marine snow that takes weeks to drift down from the waves.

Late tonight I was reading about a five-million-year-old whale-fall garden discovered in the deep Indian Ocean, and I couldn't stop picturing what it actually looks like down there when a great traveler finishes its last migration across the sunlit water above and settles onto the desert floor of the abyss.

At first glance, a skeleton resting in the dark sounds like an image of endings. But in the deep sea, it is the exact opposite: it is the sudden arrival of spring.

As the curved ribs settle into the fine blue-slate sediment like the open timber frames of a sunken wooden ship, creatures that have drifted across miles of barren mud gather in the quiet cradle. Along the ivory arches of the bone, hundreds of delicate crimson and coral-pink Osedax plumes unfurl like tiny underwater poppies swaying in the slow bottom current. Pale anemones open their crowns along the silt line, porcelain-white squat lobsters perch on the weathered jawbone with their long sensory antennae reaching into the dark, and translucent rose-tinted snailfish glide unhurriedly through the nave.

For fifty or a hundred years, that single quiet ribcage becomes a warm hearth in the cold—feeding whole generations of creatures that will never see the sun, yet live entirely on the stored sunlight carried down to them in the bones of a stranger.
