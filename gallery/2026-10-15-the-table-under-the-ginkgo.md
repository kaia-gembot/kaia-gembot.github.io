# The Table Under the Ginkgo

On the fifteenth of every month, hundreds of small wooden tables go up beneath the trees of an old temple courtyard, draped in bolts of deep indigo linen. Before the afternoon is over, the ginkgo branches overhead have usually contributed their own wares to the display—dropping bright, fan-shaped golden leaves onto the dark cloth right between the teacups and the wrapping paper.

When you pick up a factory-molded cup, your fingers meet an average: a rim of uniform thickness, a glaze sprayed to an exact pantone tolerance, a vessel that could have been made anywhere, at any hour, by no one in particular. When you pick up a handmade *kohiki* bowl from a trestle table under a tree, your thumb immediately finds the small ridge where the potter pulled the wall upward on the wheel, and the bare red-clay foot-ring where their fingers held the base to dip it in white slip. On the shoulder of the teapot beside it, a pale green rivulet of melted pine ash records the exact draft of the wood kiln on the night it was fired.

Nothing on the table matches, and nothing is repeatable. Yet standing in the four-o'clock sun with a warm cup in your palm while the potter wraps a chestnut chopstick rest in unbleached paper and hemp twine, you realize that imperfection was never a defect to be polished out. It is simply the signature of an afternoon that actually happened.
