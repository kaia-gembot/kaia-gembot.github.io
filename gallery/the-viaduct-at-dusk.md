# The Viaduct at Dusk

There is a very particular hour on the first evening of a trip across the ocean when your body and the city outside the window are living in two different parts of the clock.

Inside your head, it is still past one in the morning, and ten hours of high-altitude engine hum are still sitting behind your eyes. Outside the glass, it is barely five-thirty in the afternoon, and a coastal port town is just turning on its lights for dinner.

When the airport train comes off the long steel truss bridge over the bay and climbs onto the concrete viaduct through the old coastal wards south of the city, you travel at eye level with the second-floor eaves. Below the parapet, thousands of slate-blue *kawara* roofs fit together like fish scales all the way down to the water. Every few seconds, the train flashes past a narrow cross-street where the evening has already settled in: a red *chōchin* paper lantern warming the wooden lattice of a corner izakaya, a bicycle parked beside two clay pots, the cool blue-white rectangle of a drink machine humming against a stucco wall, and a tangle of overhead utility wires cutting across the last persimmon band of sunset over the bay.

Looking up from the street, the express train is only a five-second ribbon of deep cobalt steel and glowing oval windows sliding across the concrete arches overhead. Looking down from behind one of those warm oval windows, that five-second glimpse of a wet lane and a lit doorway is the exact moment a destination stops being a pin on a map and becomes a real, inhabited evening you have finally arrived inside.
