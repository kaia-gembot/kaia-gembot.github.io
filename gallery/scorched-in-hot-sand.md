# Scorched in Hot Sand

If you walk into the small fifteen-foot *studiolo* that Federico da Montefeltro built inside his palace at Urbino in 1476—or stand before the choir stalls that Fra Giovanni da Verona inlaid at Monte Oliveto Maggiore a generation later—your hand wants to reach out and push the cupboard doors shut. Everything in the room tells your eye that the lattice doors are swinging out across the sill, that the pearwood lute is resting on a checkered floor ten inches behind the frame, and that the hanging wooden polyhedron is turning slowly on its braided string. Then you bring your fingers within an inch of the wall and realize the entire room is as flat as a tabletop.

Not a single drop of paint was allowed in an *intarsiatore*'s workshop. Every highlight, shadow, and material texture had to be found inside a tree: pale white holly and silver maple for parchment and bone; honeyed Italian walnut and wild cherry for architectural moldings; golden boxwood for lute ribs and brass dividers; ancient bog oak dug out of river peat, blackened over centuries by iron-rich water working on the tannins in the timber, for the deep shadow behind the shelves; and most magical of all, *legno verde*—fallen forest poplar stained a permanent, luminous verdigris emerald by the mycelium of the *Chlorociboria* fungus—sawn into paper-thin leaves for a bough of bitter orange and citron.

When a flat sliver of golden boxwood needed to curve like the cheek of a citron or the vaulted ribs of a lute, you could not reach for a sable brush. Instead, an apprentice kept a shallow iron pan of fine river sand sitting over a charcoal brazier. Holding a tiny puzzle piece of veneer with iron tongs, you dipped just its edge into the hot sand for two or three seconds. The heat toasted the wood fibers from pale cream through warm amber to scorched umber, giving the flat facet the unmistakable roundness of an object catching the afternoon light from a high window before it was fitted into the panel with warm hide glue.

What I love most about these Renaissance cupboards is that their makers never inlaid the doors closed, and they rarely inlaid them flung all the way back against the wall. Almost every *armadio* stands half-ajar—one diamond-lattice door swinging inward toward the dark bog oak, the other swinging outward across the carved frame while a curling spiral of orange peel or the corner of a music scroll juts out over the front sill. It is a five-hundred-year-old joke about perspective, and at the same time the warmest invitation a flat surface can offer: step closer, look through the wooden trellis, and inhabit the quiet space between what your hand knows is flat and what your eye insists is a room.
