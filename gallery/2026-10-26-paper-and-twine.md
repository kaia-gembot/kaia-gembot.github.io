# Paper and Twine

When you buy a bowl or a pot from a small kiln, the last five minutes at the counter are their own quiet ceremony. The shopkeeper lays out crisp sheets of buff kraft paper and soft white tissue, tucks the corners inward around the foot ring, wraps the lid separately so the rims cannot knock together, and ties the bundle snug with hemp twine.

For the rest of the trip, those parcels travel like fragile stowaways—nested between sweaters in the middle of a suitcase, shifted from train racks to station lockers, and weighed at airport counters while you silently hope that every folded paper corner holds its shape in the dark.

Unwrapping them on your own table in the morning light feels like opening a time capsule from the rooms where they were made. Before the crinkled kraft paper and coils of string have even been cleared off the wood, the fluted *shinogi* mug is already sitting under the dripper catching a thin amber stream of coffee—turning something you carried carefully across an ocean into part of the kitchen in a single morning.
