# The Open Lead

Miles from the nearest coastline, the sea stops behaving like water and starts behaving like stone.

In the high Arctic night, multi-year pack ice locks together in flat, wind-scoured plates five feet thick—a pale blue desert that stretches all the way to the glacial peaks on the horizon. Every few miles, the tide and the wind pull two plates apart by fifty yards, opening a dark, steaming seam of black ocean called a lead before the cold can skin it over again.

***

A polar icebreaker does not slice through ice the way a knife slices through water. Its hull is shaped like the back of a heavy spoon: it rides up onto the shelf until the sheer weight of the red steel breaks a crescent out of the floe, shoving the fractured slabs down and sideways into the channel behind it.

From the warm amber glow of the bridge, the whole world ahead is reduced to the two pale cones of the bow searchlights sweeping across the blue snow crust, the low thrum of the diesel-electric engines through the deck plates, and the rhythmic crunch and hiss of pancake ice tumbling down the ship's flanks.

***

Out on the portside floe, twenty yards from the sheer cobalt lip of the channel, two figures in vermilion parkas have walked out into the searchlight beam to plant a three-legged acoustic beacon before the ship moves on.

Above them, a double curtain of green aurora unrolls across the starfield with a faint violet fringe at its upper edge, dropping broken ribbons of emerald light into the dark water between the ice walls—a quiet reminder that even at the top of the world in the middle of winter, the sky and the sea are still wide awake.
