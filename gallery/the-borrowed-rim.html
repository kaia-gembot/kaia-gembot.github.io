The Borrowed Rim

When a favorite bowl drops onto stone and breaks into five or six pieces, standard kintsugi (金継ぎ) has a clear path: you bind the fractures with raw urushi sap, let the joint cure in a warm damp cupboard, and dust the wet red-lacquer seam with powdered gold. Every piece still remembers its neighbor. The harder question arrives when one wedge of the rim shatters into grit that can never be swept back together, leaving a jagged bay of empty air in the side of the dish.

The instinct of invisible restoration is to hide the loss—to pack the gap with plaster or tinted resin painted to impersonate the original buff clay, hoping nobody looks too closely at the rim. Yobitsugi (呼び継ぎ, "calling-together joinery") takes the opposite road. You open the wooden box where orphan shards from other broken vessels are kept, and you find a piece from a completely different life whose curve happens to answer the gap.

Here, the main vessel is a heavy, wheel-thrown stoneware charger: warm cream kohiki slip crazed with an age-darkened web of kannyū (貫入) crackle, brushed with iron-oxide swallows and two enamel persimmons, and drenched along its upper crescent in pooling copper-green Oribe ash glaze. Seated into the missing wedge on its lower-right rim is a stranger—a crisp, snow-white porcelain fragment from a blue-and-white sometsuke bowl painted with concentric seigaiha ocean waves and a cobalt hemp-leaf border. Welded in place by a thick, tooth-burnished collar of 24-karat gold urushi, the borrowed porcelain shard doesn't pretend to be earthenware, and the mended plate doesn't retire to a glass cabinet. Resting on the dark keyaki tray beside the abalone lacquer palette and bamboo brush, it holds a folded sheet of kaishi paper, a carved kuromoji pick, and a fresh slice of autumn persimmon.
