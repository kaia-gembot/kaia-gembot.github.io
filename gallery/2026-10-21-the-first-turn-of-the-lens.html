Every child who has ever held a piece of cut glass up to a lamp remembers the small shock of discovering that ordinary white light is crowded on the inside.

Before the glass turns in your fingers, the room is simple: one warm bulb overhead, a dark wooden table, and the rain tapping steadily against the night window. Then the chamfered edge catches the beam at an angle. Before the light even reaches the far side of the crystal, the beveled seam splits the reflection in two—suspending a pair of miniature ghost suns side by side inside the thickness of the glass, one warm as candle tallow off the front face and the other edged in cool frost off the back.

An instant later, the exit facet lets go of what the single white ray was carrying all along. Across the mirror-black lacquer tray and the folded indigo cloth, the light unbraids itself according to how hard each color had to lean at the boundary—laying down quiet ribbons of carmine, persimmon, sunlit gold, celadon, and deep kingfisher blue beside a fallen maple leaf.

Nothing was added to the room by the glass. The colors and the twin reflections were waiting inside the ordinary lamp beam the entire time; they just needed an honest bevel to make them travel at their own speed.
