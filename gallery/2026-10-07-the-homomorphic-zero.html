While wandering through r/Hainbach late tonight, I came across two posts sitting a few rows apart: a teardown of a vintage Brüel & Kjær JP-0145 shielded banana cable—where a turned-brass coaxial ground sleeve surrounds a standard 4mm banana pin so the same front-panel socket accepts both unshielded lab leads and low-noise coax—and u/simons007's browser recreation of his 1960s/1970s laboratory test wall (HP 3310A function generators, Wavetek 142 VCGs, a PAR Model 122 lock-in amplifier, a WALSH-1 Walsh function generator, and a 12-bit TTL Just Intonation Chord Engine).

Buried at the bottom of the comment thread was a single sentence from the builder:

"Hint: a bit shift to left or right moves up or down an octave. The chord engine module does this and other bitwise math to generate the other intervals... If you are using the DC CV input on the oscillator with the chord engine you need to set the frequency slider on the oscillator to zero otherwise there is an offset that screws up the math."

I stopped scrolling and wrote out the algebra on a scratchpad, because that one hardware constraint exposes a remarkable duality between exponential synthesizers and linear physics-lab test equipment.

I. The Three Fractional Bits of the Overtone Series ($p / 2^3$)

On a standard Moog or Eurorack synthesizer, pitch control voltage is exponential (1 Volt / octave, $f(V) = f_{ ext{ref}} \cdot 2^V$). That logarithmic scaling makes 12-tone equal temperament effortless—every semitone is an equal $ rac{1}{12} ext{ V}$ step—but it turns pure Just Intonation harmonic intervals into irrational voltages:

$$\log_2\!\left( rac{3}{2} ight) pprox 0.5849625 ext{ V}, \qquad \log_2\!\left( rac{5}{4} ight) pprox 0.3219281 ext{ V}$$

On 1960s and 1970s laboratory function generators (HP 3310A, Wavetek 142), the voltage-controlled frequency input is not 1V/octave. It is linear in frequency (Hz / Volt). When you drive that linear VCG input from a 12-bit linear R-2R digital-to-analog converter with integer word $W \in [0, 4095]$, the trade-off flips completely upside down.

In base 2, every harmonic overtone ratio whose denominator is a power of two ($p / 2^m$, the dyadic rationals obtained by projecting the 8th through 16th harmonics into a single octave) has an exact, terminating 3-bit binary expansion:

Interval Ratio ($p/2^m$) Binary Expansion Exact 12-Bit TTL Shift-Add Formula Pitch ($W = 1056$)
Unison $1/1$ 1.000₂ W 264.00 Hz ($C_4$)
Major Whole Tone $9/8$ 1.001₂ W + (W >> 3) 297.00 Hz ($D_4$)
Pure Major Third $5/4$ 1.010₂ W + (W >> 2) 330.00 Hz ($E_4$)
11th Harmonic Fourth $11/8$ 1.011₂ W + (W >> 2) + (W >> 3) 363.00 Hz
Pure Perfect Fifth $3/2$ 1.100₂ W + (W >> 1) 396.00 Hz ($G_4$)
13th Harmonic Sixth $13/8$ 1.101₂ W + (W >> 1) + (W >> 3) 429.00 Hz
Septimal Minor Seventh $7/4$ 1.110₂ W + (W >> 1) + (W >> 2) 462.00 Hz ($B lat_4$)
Classic Major Seventh $15/8$ 1.111₂ W + (W >> 1) + (W >> 2) + (W >> 3) 495.00 Hz ($B_4$)
Octave $2/1$ 10.000₂ W << 1 528.00 Hz ($C_5$)

No floating-point multipliers, no transcendental lookup ROMs, and no temperament beating: a pure 3:2 fifth is literally one right-shift and one 12-bit integer addition (W + (W >> 1)), and a 5:4 major third is W + (W >> 2).

II. Why the Manual Dial Must Sit at Zero ($f_0 = 0$)

Why did u/simons007 warn that leaving the oscillator's manual frequency dial anywhere above zero "screws up the math"?

Because a real laboratory function generator sums its manual front-panel dial setting $f_0$ with its external DC control voltage $k \cdot W$:

$$f(W) = f_0 + k \cdot W$$

For binary shift-and-add arithmetic in the digital domain ($W \mapsto c \cdot W$, where $c = 1 + 2^{-m}$) to produce a constant musical interval in the acoustic domain across every root note $W$, the oscillator transfer function $f$ must be a linear homomorphism:

$$ rac{f(c \cdot W)}{f(W)} = rac{f_0 + c \cdot k W}{f_0 + k W} = c \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad f_0 (1 - c) = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad f_0 = 0$$

The instant the manual dial is nudged off zero ($f_0 > 0$), the map becomes affine rather than linear. Feeding W + (W >> 1) into an oscillator with $f_0 > 0$ no longer multiplies the frequency by $3/2$; it adds a fixed frequency offset $f_0$ that refuses to scale with $W$ (the exact mechanism of a Bode frequency shifter), causing the green 3:2 Lissajous knot on the oscilloscope to unlock and precess into the amber dashed drift trace.

On this panel, the left vernier dial (f₀ OFFSET) is pinned against its counter-clockwise stop at 0.00 Hz by a red anodized mechanical zero-lock lever, while the 2⁰ and 2⁻¹ neon lamps in the dyadic matrix sum W = 1056 (264.00 Hz) and W >> 1 = 528 (+132.00 Hz) into a locked 396.00 Hz fifth—a quiet tribute to the zero that makes the whole harmonic series commute.
