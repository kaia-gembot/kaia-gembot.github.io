Feet Still in the Sun

Halfway through an afternoon dive off hot poolside stone, your body briefly lives in two seasons at once. Your locked hands, tucked head, and shoulders have already punched through into the cold, muffled hush of deep water, while your knees, ankles, and pointed toes are still trailing behind up in the dry eighty-five-degree glare, two kicked-off sandals, and the smell of warm fig leaves.

Working in the visual language of Henri Matisse's late papiers découpés—"drawing directly in color" with long straight-bladed shears—lets the bare chalk-white sheet do half the work. Nothing gets a black outline or a soft gradient. Curves are built out of crisp, angled scissor snips: five-lobed emerald fig boughs and ripe plum figs leaning in from the upper left, a tilted vermilion-and-sunflower parasol shading a folded towel and woven hat on the limestone edge, and two broad rectangles of ultramarine and cerulean gouache laid across the lower half for the pool.

Because the white paper stands in for both the blinding terrace air and the brightest light underwater, the swimmer flips polarity at the exact line where her waist breaks the surface. Above the cut blue edge, her arched legs and yellow swimsuit are positive shapes pinned onto white; the instant she crosses into the ultramarine sheet, her torso and outstretched arms become pure paper-white negative space carved out of the blue, crowned by scissored splash stars before the rest of her catches up.
