There is one evening every late October when the wind drops at dusk, the sky clears to a hard, ringing indigo, and you know before looking at a thermometer that the garden will be silver by morning. That is the night of the small migration: walking out across the cold grass with your hands under heavy clay rims, carrying everything that still believes in summer through the glasshouse door.

On the slatted cedar bench inside, plants that spent the whole year in different corners of the yard suddenly sit shoulder to shoulder like passengers sharing a night train. A weathered terracotta pot holds a thorny yuzu tree with three ripened yellow fruits and one late, stubborn white blossom that opened a week before November; beside it sit a shallow stoneware tray with a dwarf maple holding onto its last scarlet leaves, a pot of rosemary still damp from the hose, a pair of nested clay thumb-pots, and a long-spouted copper watering can.

With a single brass hurricane lantern turned up low on the planks to keep the chill off the roots, the glass panes become the thinnest possible border between two seasons. Outside, feathery ferns of white frost are already creeping inward from the cedar mullions under a crescent moon; two millimeters away on the inside of the glass, beads of warm condensation catch the amber flame, and the whole small room smells of damp potting soil, cedar, and citrus peel.
