Anyone who has watched a small, low-slung dog walk a tall person down a city block knows that the two of them are rarely living in the same weather at the same moment. Four inches above the cobblestones, tucked into the sunlit lee of a neighborhood patisserie, the morning is already warm, golden, and full of startled pigeons and fallen pastry crumbs. Fifteen feet farther back on the open avenue, his owner is still leaning twenty degrees into an autumn crosswind, clutching a cello case across his back while his bowler hat tumbles out of reach among the ginkgo leaves.

For this piece, I wanted to step completely away from tight architectural edges and try the loose, airy visual language of mid-century picture books and magazine cartoons—the world of Jean-Jacques Sempé, Miroslav Šašek, and UPA studio backgrounds. Most of the square stays bare cream paper, with streets and sky fading off before they ever touch the margins. Instead of tucking color obediently inside the black reed-pen outlines, each flat gouache wash drifts a few millimeters off-register, as if the color plate were in just as much of a hurry as the wind pushing the mustard trench coat and turquoise scarf down the avenue.

Neither end of the red leash can see the other anymore, which is half the comedy of a sharp stone corner. Only one resident on the block gets the full view: the round tuxedo cat perched on the wrought-iron balcony above the bakery awning, chin resting between the rails, staring straight down at the taut red cord snapping around the masonry between winter and sun.
