In most cities, a restaurant wants the ground-floor corner with the widest plate-glass window on the block, so anyone walking past on the sidewalk can look in and see every table at a glance.

In Tokyo's oldest wooden lanes, a whole kitchen and ten counter stools will happily fold themselves into the second-floor attic of a building barely wider than a doorway. Down at street level, there is no dining room at all—only a paper chōchin lantern glowing warm red against the cedar siding, a short indigo noren brushed soft at shoulder height, and a flight of wooden stairs so steep and narrow that you climb it with one hand on the polished rail like a ship's companionway.

All the life is ten feet overhead. When you pause on the wet flagstones after an autumn shower, the room upstairs gives itself away before you ever set foot on the bottom step: the square of golden tungsten light propped open under the eaves, the clatter of porcelain bowls and bamboo noodle baskets, and the slow, fragrant cloud of broth steam curling out past the utility wires while its reflection shimmers upside down among the fallen ginkgo leaves at your feet.
