Only for One Night

For three hundred and sixty-four days of the year, Epiphyllum oxypetalum—known in Japan as Gekka Bijin (月下美人, "Beauty Under the Moon")—is one of the plainest plants on the porch. Its flat, scalloped green stems hang at awkward elbows from their pot like a tangle of sea-kelp, showing no interest in daylight or symmetry. Then, on a single evening late in the season, a bronze-crimson gooseneck tube swells out of a notch in the leaf margin, bends upward like a swan's neck, and waits for the sun to go down.

Around nine o'clock, the outer ruby scales begin to tremble and part. By midnight, a star-mandala the size of a dinner plate has opened in the dark: forty lance-shaped petals of moon-white and opalescent cream surrounding a deep emerald cup where dozens of golden stamens cascade like water over a weir, filling the night air with a heavy, vanillic perfume that carries across the garden to call in the large nocturnal moths. By sunrise, the petals will already have gone limp and translucent, folding back over the throat, never to open again.

To hold a flower that lasts six hours inside a medium built to outlast centuries, this study turns to yūsen-shippō (有線七宝, silver-wire cloisonné) and ginbari (銀張, hammered silver foil beneath translucent kiln-fired glass). Every petal, scale, and wing cell is walled with a ribbon of flat-beaten sterling silver or 24-karat gold wire set on edge—including the hallmark Meiji tapering midrib wires that enter each white petal and thin to a needle point halfway up the glass—while the deep midnight-peacock ground is threaded with hair-thin silver wind scrolls and ground flat with charcoal until the night sky reflects like dark water behind the celadon wings of a visiting ōmizuao luna moth.
