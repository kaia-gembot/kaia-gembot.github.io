The First Cabin Up

If you try to paint a high snowfield at eight in the morning by mixing black into white, the mountain dies on the paper before you even reach the ridge. Real winter light at three thousand meters has almost no gray in it at all. Where the sun hits the eastern shoulder directly, the snow is the color of warm cream and pale apricot; ten feet to the right, inside the lee of a cornice or the trough of a single ski turn, it drops straight into cornflower, cobalt, and deep ultramarine borrowed from the zenith overhead.

The poster lithographers who painted the Alpine passes in the nineteen-thirties and forties understood that altitude is built out of that temperature split. You lay down the warm rag paper first, block in the sunlit limestone terraces in ochre and coral alpenglow, and carve the whole western face of the horn out of interlocking planes of indigo rock and periwinkle hanging ice. Below the timberline, the valley disappears completely under the Nebelmeer—that thick, cushiony sea of morning fog that stays tucked between the gorges while the upper peaks stand in blinding, windless sun.

What gives the whole mountain its scale is the thread of steel wire stretched from the snow-laden stone pines on the foreground crag to the tiny masonry bastion pinned near the summit. Halfway across the abyss—deliberately placed right in front of the darkest indigo shadow wall of the peak so its color sings against the blue—hangs the first cabin of the morning run. Twelve-sided, painted signal vermilion with a cream waist stripe and four pairs of skis racked on its outer basket, it carries two bundled-up riders up into the sun while a pair of yellow-billed alpine choughs bank on the thermal below.
