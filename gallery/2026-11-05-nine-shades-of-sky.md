# Nine Shades of Sky

If you have never stood beside an indigo vat in the morning, nobody can quite convince you that blue begins as green.

Down inside the sunken earthenware jars, the dye bath has been resting under its cedar lids all night—warm, alkaline, and fed with a splash of sake and wheat bran so the bacteria keep breathing through the cold November dark. When the wooden cover comes off and the surface is gently drawn back with a bamboo paddle, a cushion of iridescent violet-and-copper foam gathers at the center like a dark metallic flower. Yet the liquid underneath is not blue at all; it is a quiet, amber-tinted yellow-green that smells of wet earth, wood ash, and autumn leaves.

The transformation happens entirely in the air between the rim of the jar and the bamboo pole overhead. You lift a skein of cotton out of the bath and for the first five seconds it is the color of young moss in April. Then the mountain wind hits the wet threads, oxygen finds the reduced indigo waiting inside the fiber, and you can literally watch the cloth drink the sky—jade darkening into teal, teal deepening into kingfisher blue, and twenty dips later settling into a midnight navy so dark it holds a faint bronze sheen in the sun.

Across the courtyard poles, five bolts of cloth hang side by side like five different hours of the same day: the palest single-dip *kamenozoki* ("peeking into the jar") catching the white morning light on the left, the spider-web *shibori* resist rings glowing in the middle, and the newest bolt on the right still dripping green back into the vat while a late swallow banks past the eaves.
